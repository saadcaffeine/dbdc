## Research and prior art

| Work | Why it matters to DBDC |
|---|---|
| **NIST rotating-wheel braille display** (Roberts, Slattery and Kardos, 2000) | Moved the dots onto the rim of a spinning wheel, set by stationary actuators, so the reader's finger can stay still. The aim was a tenfold cost reduction compared with 80-cell displays then costing $10,000–15,000. DBDC shares the idea of putting the braille on a wheel, but uses one wheel per cell and encodes the patterns in the cam. [NIST publication](https://www.nist.gov/node/710926), [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refreshable_braille_display) |
| **Jacques Mattheij's braille display notes** | Detailed open notes on low-cost mechanisms, including odometer-style wheels and a 64-position wheel covering all six-dot codes, with a target of about $5 per cell. Very relevant reading. [Notes](https://jacquesmattheij.com/refreshablebraille/BrailleDisplayProject.html) |
| **de Bruijn sequences** | The combinatorial structure that lets one rim hold every pattern exactly once. DBDC uses B(4,3) generated by the Fredricksen–Kessler–Maiorana algorithm. |

Orbit Reader 20 and Braille Me show that cheaper cell mechanisms can bring 20-cell displays under about $520. Canute shows that multi-line mechanical displays can cost under £7 per cell. DBDC explores the same space with open hardware that can be made locally.
